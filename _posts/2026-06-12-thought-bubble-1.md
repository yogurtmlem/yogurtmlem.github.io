# Thought bubble 1. Learning Japanese
Hi, I decided that learning Japanese would be the topic of my first blog post. I'll briefly talk about why I started learning Japanese and we'll see where it goes from there I don't have anything planned beforehand.

Hazy Moon by Hatsune Miku + Kodai Fujimoto. The first one is probably what gave me the idea, the later one is the push that i needed to finally start learning. The next line is only for Kodai Fujimoto, if you're not Kodai Fujimoto, skip ahead.  こだいくん、最近忙しくてたくさんお金を持っているはずですね。三億円をお願いします。現金で。

## How
The first hurdle is learning the alphabets, Hiragana and Katakana. This took me around 2 weeks to really get them down. [Tofugu](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese/learn-hiragana/) is a great source, their image-based mnemonics are helpful for those who are not familiar with non-Latin writing systems. I recommend drilling the [Tofugu’s Learn Kana Quiz](https://kana-quiz.tofugu.com) until you get only 1 or 2 wrong.  

Next, you can either use an app, pdf scans of a textbook or the physical version to start learning. Call me old fashioned but I firmly believe it's best to start out with physical textbooks, that extra step of buying a book helped me stay commited after the honeymoon phase had passed. That said, any method works fine, it all comes down to whether or not you can turn it into a habit. I picked Minna no Nihongo 1 and 2 because that was the first thing that caught my eye when I googled "Japanese books for beginners Reddit", plus it was on sale on Shopee so buying it was a no brainer. There are a lot of books in the MNN serie but I recommend buying the main book + the translation and the exercise book. Free lessons posted on Youtube cover pretty much everything you need to **pass** the N3.
> ***Do not do what I did***. Speaking from personal experience, Minna no Nihongo is great if your goal is to pass the JLPT + have a strong grasp on Japanese grammar structures + have a solid vocabulary. If your goal is, for example, to be able to hold a conversation in Japanese within xx months, other books like Marugoto would be better suited for you (or so I've heard).

I usually need 2 days to get through one lesson, one for learning grammar and vocabulary the other for doing exercise and drilling flashcards (~3 hours/day). Day 1's workload is more time consuming compared to Day 2. The Youtube lectures I watched are (1) a series of videos aims to introduce vocabulary - provide example and guidance for correct pronunciation; (2) a series of videos aims to break down grammar - explain use cases for each structure. For the first day, watch the vocab video to familiarize yourself with today's topic--> make flashcards (by far the most painstaking part of the process by a wide margin) --> learn by heart the vocabulary of that lesson (including the supplemental vocabulary section) --> watch the grammar video --> write everything down on a notebook.  

Day 2 is more straightforward, do all the exercise, use a highlighter to circle your mistakes, write down an explanation for each mistake, try to teach it to yourself is what I'm trying to say. This works 80% of the time but with Japanese the difference between similar structures can get a bit blurred, I suggest you should try to figure it out yourself first then post your reasoning on the r/LearnJapanese subreddit to check for accuracy or ask a teacher if you want to hear a more nuanced and detailed explaination. At least that's what I've been doing. 

For flashcards, I went through my options: Quizlet, Anki, Kahoot,... Quizlet has a lot of useful features locked behind a paywall, same case applied to Kahoot which left me with Anki. It's free, have built-in space repitition function with a wide range of add-ons. With Anki you can download pre-made vocabulary decks from Anki community or make them yourself, I opted for the second option. I learn kanjis purely using flashcards so I can read them but can only write a few basic ones. Since I only remember the general shape of each kanji, I don't concern myself with strokes order either. As a result I risk getting kanjis with similar looking radicals mixed up, the only tip I can give here is to drill the Sino-Vietnamese meaning of each kanji into your head. There's pretty much no other way around it. Another thing is the process of creating flashcards, as a beginner you'll need to watch a couple of tutorials to get used to Anki, don't sweat the details since you can always fine tune your set-up as you navigate through various add-ons later. I spent about 2-3 days figuring this out, lots and lots of trials and errors.

For listening practice, I stick to MNN kaiwa recordings and the listening workbook. Japanese is spoken at [**~7.84 syllables**](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/235971274_A_cross-Language_Perspective_on_Speech_Information_Rate) per second, every conversation is fast and heavily context-based. There are times when I find myself listening but not able to retain any information, especially when it involves arithmetic operations or just numbers in general. Yea I don't know how to write a conclusion for this section I still struggle with listening.

> Small anecdote: I originally planned to learn Japanese using English since I'd taken notice of my declining ability to think and write in English. 10 lessons in and I could no longer ignore the fact that it is objectively better to learn Japanese using Vietnamese (both are Sinosphere languages with a lot of Chinese loan words) so I made the switch. This was extremely on the nose I was just stubborn and pretentious.

## The elephant in the room
I have touched on everything under the sun accept for one thing, speaking. Speaking is a very, how should I put this, particular skill that can not be forcefully developed within a short period of time, unlike passive skills like reading or listening. Input does not always equal output type of shit. My short term goal is to pass the N2 JLPT (ideally with 170 points or up) and luckily speaking has yet to be integrated into the official test so for the foreseeable future, I don't see myself putting too much time into refining it. Another thing is that I have a penchant for overanalyzing my mistakes ie. feel the need to run every sentence through a grammar checker before saying it out loud for fear of using the wrong structure/inappropriate form, you can see why this mentality hinders my ability to speak Japanese comfortably. I will put a pin on this and circle back to it later when I've achieved my short term goal.
